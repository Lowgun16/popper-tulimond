**THE WHY**

When you were twelve, you knew exactly who you were going to be.

Out past dark with your friends. Building forts, racing bikes, certain the life ahead of you would be an adventure — and certain of the man you'd be when you got there.

Then you woke up an adult, and the dreams got traded for responsibilities. They didn't die. You just got good at turning the volume down, drowning them out with everything else.

Here's a question that never left me: if the twelve-year-old you and the eighty-year-old you both got to meet the man you are right now — what would they think?

Those are the only two judges who matter. One was all dream and certainty, sure of who you'd become. The other gets the last look back — and he's either at peace with the life you handed him, or he isn't. Between them is now. This. There's no second youth waiting on the other side of getting old. You don't get another one of these.

And you are going to meet that old man. Sooner than you think. The only thing left to decide is whether he's proud of how you spent it — or whether you spent it quiet, careful, and forgettable, so as not to bother anyone.

That's the trap. Somewhere we all got handed the same four shirts as every other man and told it didn't matter — we're all the same anyway. So we dressed the same. And when you dress like everyone, you start to move like everyone, and you disappear into everyone. You cannot live a life worth remembering as a copy of the man standing next to you.

I don't believe you're the same as everyone else. That's the whole reason this exists.

When I was twelve, the men I looked up to were nothing alike — Arnold Schwarzenegger, Billy Idol, Jose Canseco. Cool and tough for completely different reasons. That was the point. They were unapologetically themselves, and the world remembered them for it. No two of us are the same, no matter how badly the world wants us to be.

Popper Tulimond means "not for everyone." It isn't a slogan. It's a reminder: you don't owe the crowd your life. You owe it to two people — the boy who believed in what you could become, and the old man who'll remember how close you got.

So live for them, not for the room. Remember the man that twelve-year-old was so sure you'd become, and decide — today — to be him.

He isn't for everyone. He's for you.

And so are we.
